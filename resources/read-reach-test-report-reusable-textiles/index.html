Buyer document review guide

How to Read a REACH-Related Test Report for Reusable Textiles

A laboratory report is evidence about identified samples and tests. To decide whether it helps with a REACH question, match its sample, materials, analytes, restrictions, method, limits and results to the exact product and market context.

Read the report as a record of a defined test

Record the report ID and date, laboratory, sample ID and description, tested materials, analytes or restrictions, methods, reporting limits, results and stated scope. Then compare the tested sample with your ordered SKU. REACH is a regulatory framework, not a product certificate; a laboratory report alone does not establish blanket compliance.

Define the question before judging the report

“REACH tested” is too vague for a purchasing decision. First identify the destination market, product type, materials and specific concern. Is the request about a particular restricted substance, a material, an article, a coating or a buyer’s restricted-substance list? Ask which restriction or requirement is relevant and what evidence the buyer expects. The report can only be interpreted against a defined question.

REACH requirements and restrictions are specific to substances, uses, articles and circumstances. ECHA publishes information about substances restricted under REACH. The applicable entry and current conditions need to be checked for the actual product and supply chain. This article does not determine legal applicability or declare any particular product compliant.

Start with report identity and dates

Locate the complete report identifier, issue date, issuing laboratory and, where relevant, the laboratory’s accreditation details. Check that the copy is complete, legible and final rather than a quotation, draft or partial page. If a report has appendices, verify that the sample description and results refer to the same report number and revision. A logo or accreditation mark is not a substitute for confirming the relevant test capability and scope with the laboratory when that matters to your process.

Note sample receipt, preparation and test dates if given. A report records testing of a sample at a particular time; it does not necessarily state an expiry date or represent later production. Do not invent a validity period when none is stated. Ask the laboratory or supplier whether the report remains representative after material, process, supplier or product changes.

Match sample identity to the finished SKU

Read the sample identification section carefully: client, item or model code, material name, color, batch or lot, sample description, number of specimens, and any client-provided descriptions. Determine who selected and supplied the sample, whether the laboratory sampled it independently or received it from a client, and whether the report identifies that distinction. A lab’s report of a submitted sample does not, by itself, prove that every production lot is identical to that sample.

For a reusable pad, compare the tested article with the face fabric, absorbent layers, waterproof backing, thread, binding, closure, print and finish in the ordered construction. For a diaper, include shell or cover, insert, elastics, snaps, lamination, trim and print where relevant. If the report names only a fabric, its findings should not automatically be described as applying to the assembled product. A component or material certificate or report does not automatically cover a finished product.

Ask the supplier to map each report sample to a bill of materials and product revision. If one report covers only selected colors or materials, establish which variants were represented and why that selection is considered relevant. Do not assume that a similar-looking print, alternate mill, new finish or substitute component is equivalent without a documented assessment.

Check analytes, restrictions and test methods

Find the report’s list of substances or analytes and the stated regulatory or buyer criteria. Confirm that the list corresponds to the buyer’s question and the current restriction scope. A panel described as “REACH” may cover only selected substances; do not infer that every potentially relevant restriction was included. Ask the laboratory or compliance specialist to identify gaps between the requested scope and the report’s actual test panel.

For each analyte, note the method and version, sample preparation, units, reporting or quantification limit, and any deviations or qualifications. If the report states “not detected,” determine the detection or reporting limit and the tested sample. “Not detected” does not mean zero. A numerical result must be read with its unit and the relevant threshold, and the threshold itself must be the correct one for the identified substance and use.

Reports sometimes show a result as pass or fail, but the buyer should still identify the pass criterion used. Check whether the criterion is a legal limit, a customer specification or another reference. If the laboratory marks the result as outside the scope of accreditation or uses a client-provided specification, record that boundary. Ask for clarification if the report does not identify its comparison rule.

Interpret results with their boundaries

Review every result alongside the sample and method. Note whether results apply to one material, a composite sample or a finished article, and whether compositing could affect interpretation of an individual component. Look for remarks about sample condition, exclusions, subcontracted tests, deviations, uncertainty or limitations on use. Do not generalize one sample result to products that were not identified or tested.

When a report says a tested sample met named criteria, preserve the wording and boundaries. Avoid converting that narrow statement into “REACH certified,” “fully REACH compliant” or a product-wide guarantee. REACH is not a certificate scheme for a finished textile product, and no single test report can establish every obligation that might apply to all substances, uses, supply-chain roles and future production.

Control changes and repeat orders

Create a link between the report and the controlled product specification: SKU, revision, bill of materials, supplier or mill, colors, process and production lot where available. At each change, compare the new configuration with the tested sample. Changes to fiber source, dye, print, coating, lamination, finish, thread, fastener or supplier can affect whether the evidence remains representative. Ask a qualified compliance adviser or laboratory whether additional testing or review is warranted; do not decide that a change is immaterial solely because the product looks unchanged.

For repeat orders, verify that the same materials and approved construction are being used and determine how production lots are traceable. Set a review trigger for changed materials, updated restrictions or a buyer’s revised test panel. Keep superseded reports with their associated product revisions so a reviewer can see which evidence was current for each order.

Buyer review checklist

  • Report number, revision, issue date, laboratory and sample receipt or test dates.
  • Sample code, source, description, lot and whether the sample is a component, composite or finished article.
  • Materials and product revision represented; differences from the order are documented.
  • Analytes or restrictions listed and whether they answer the buyer’s defined question.
  • Method and version, units, reporting limits, criteria, results, deviations and exclusions.
  • Accreditation scope checked where it matters; subcontracting and limitations understood.
  • Change-control owner, repeat-order traceability and next review trigger recorded.

Request a report with product context

When asking a supplier for documentation, name the target product revision, destination and substance or buyer requirement. Request the report’s full sample description and relevant result pages, plus a written mapping from the report sample to the proposed SKU. Ask which materials and variants were included, what the report did not test, which limits were applied, and how the supplier controls changes after testing. If information is confidential, agree a suitable way to share enough identifying detail for the buyer to assess scope.

This guide provides a method for reviewing documents, not a legal conclusion or a claim about ReuseCare products. No claim is made that every SKU is REACH-compliant. Claims depend on the exact holder, scope, validity and ordered SKU, along with the applicable requirements. A component or material report does not automatically cover the finished product. See compliance information, certification information, and the materials and layers guide. For a specific product document request, provide the intended style and specification through Request a Quote; product starting points include reusable pads and cloth diapers.
