GOTS and GRS Scope Certificates vs Transaction Certificates

A scope certificate describes a certified organization’s approved activities and product scope. A transaction certificate (TC) is shipment- or transaction-specific evidence. Buyers need to understand both, and match records to the actual goods and supply chain.

Two documents, different questions

A current scope certificate is about an organization, its certified site or activities, and the products and processes within the scheme scope. A transaction certificate concerns specified goods moving through a particular transaction. One does not replace the other: check organization eligibility and then request the applicable goods-level record for the shipment when the scheme and transaction require it.

What a scope certificate tells a buyer

GOTS and GRS are supply-chain certification standards with their own rules, terminology and certification processes. A scope certificate (often abbreviated SC) identifies a certified entity and describes approved operations and product categories within its defined scope. It can help a buyer determine whether a company and activity appear in the certified chain. It does not, by itself, prove that a particular purchase order or shipment consists of certified goods.

Start by checking the issuing certification body’s record or the scheme’s official verification resources, where available. Confirm the entity’s legal name, site or facility information, certificate number, standard and version, product categories, processing or trading activities, and current status and validity dates. Compare the actual role of the named entity—such as processor, manufacturer, trader or supplier—with the step it performed for your goods. Similar names across affiliates do not establish that the relevant site or legal entity is included.

Then examine product scope. Broad category language may cover a class of goods but leave the exact article or material unclear. Ask the supplier or certification body to map the offered product to the recorded category and to identify any exclusions. A scope certificate that lists an organization and a category is not equivalent to a finished-product approval for every SKU made, sourced or sold by that organization.

What a transaction certificate adds

A transaction certificate is tied to a transaction and the goods described in that record. Depending on scheme rules and the transaction, it may identify seller and buyer, shipment or invoice references, product description, quantity, applicable standard, certificate identifier, dates and related supply-chain parties. The buyer should use the original or verifiable record supplied through the relevant certification process, not a generic example or a copied certificate number.

Use the record to connect the goods to the purchase and shipment. Compare product descriptions and codes with the purchase order and invoice; quantities and units with packing lists; shipment dates and references with shipping records; and the relevant parties with the supply-chain documents. Review lot or batch references if present. If the TC does not state a lot code, ask the supplier how the transaction is linked to the identified goods and what traceability record supports that link.

A discrepancy is a question to resolve, not something to edit away. For example, a different legal entity, a quantity mismatch, a product description that names only raw material, or a date outside the apparent transaction sequence may need clarification from the supplier or certification body. Record the answer with supporting documentation and escalate material gaps before accepting a claim for the goods.

Apply the distinction to GOTS and GRS

Do not assume that GOTS and GRS use identical eligibility, product, chain-of-custody, transaction-document or claim rules. Review the current version of the relevant standard and implementation guidance for the product and transaction in question. The GOTS Standard describes GOTS requirements and certification concepts. Textile Exchange publishes a current scope and transaction certificate policy and an official certificate authentication service. Confirm the applicable standard version and verification route with the certification body for the document and order.

For either scheme, a supplier’s scope certificate can be useful evidence about the supplier’s certification status and listed scope; it is not proof that an individual shipment was certified under that scheme. A transaction certificate should not be read as covering goods, product descriptions, parties or quantities beyond what it identifies. Read the actual record rather than relying on a supplier statement such as “we are GOTS/GRS certified.”

Component evidence is not finished-product evidence

Textile products are assembled from multiple materials and operations. A certified fiber, yarn, fabric, insert or other component does not automatically establish that the completed diaper or reusable pad is covered by the same claim. Finished-product status depends on the applicable scheme rules and the certified chain, product scope, processing steps and evidence for the ordered SKU. Identify all relevant layers and components in the bill of materials; ask the certification body or responsible scheme contact how they are treated.

Also distinguish a document about an organization’s scope from permission to make a product claim or use a logo. Confirm scheme claim rules, any required approval, and that the exact wording and artwork are supported. A transaction record for a raw material cannot simply be relabeled as evidence for a finished branded item.

Buyer checklist for shipment and lot records

  • Identify the scheme and current standard version; record the certificate body and number.
  • Verify each relevant supply-chain organization, site, activity, product category, status and validity.
  • Request transaction evidence applicable to the goods and shipment, where required and available under scheme rules.
  • Match seller, buyer, goods description, SKU, quantity, units, invoice or shipment reference, dates and lot or batch identifiers.
  • Ask how finished-product assembly and every applicable processing step fit the scheme’s scope.
  • Resolve missing, inconsistent or ambiguous fields with the issuer or certification body; preserve the response and traceability records.
  • Verify claim and logo rules separately before publishing an on-product or marketing statement.

Plan the evidence request before ordering

At quotation stage, tell the supplier which scheme and product claim you are considering, destination market, SKU, intended finished construction and the evidence your buyer or customer requires. Ask what can be supplied for the actual order, which legal entities and sites are involved, whether shipment-specific documents can be requested, who applies for them and when they are issued. Agree how the production lot will be identified so later documents can be matched to delivered goods.

Do not assume a TC exists before it is supplied and verified. This article makes no claim that any ReuseCare SKU has a transaction certificate or that every product is covered by GOTS or GRS. A component or material certificate does not automatically cover a finished product. Claims depend on the exact holder, scheme scope, validity, transaction evidence and ordered SKU.
